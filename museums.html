Museums in Hamburg

Museum Visited Review
Kunsthalle 31.10.24 Visited on the "see for free" day of free entry so it was very busy, and made the mistake of trying to see everything. But the collection is impressive and laid out chronologically, with the main collection beginning with medieval Christian art and progressing through to the 20th century. I particularly liked the section on impressionism, where a former curator had brought leading impressionists to Hamburg to paint local scenes. Many of these were of shipping work around the Elbe, and I found the depictions of these quotidian work-life scenes in impressionist paintings really interesting. There was also some Munch which was another highlight for me. I was less enamoured with the more recent modern art in the annex. The headline piece, Caspar David Friedrich's ''Wanderer,'' was out on loan. For this, and to digest the collection in a more leisurely way, the collection bears returning to. Luckily there is a day of late- and free-opening once a month.
MARKK 31.10.24 The Museum am Rotherbaum, also called MARKK, is an anthropological museum. It is interesting not just for its collections, but also in a meta sense as reflecting current changes in curation as decolonial approaches become more important. What is interesting about the museum is that it has several galleries which were themselves curated in different historical moments, and thus frame the material in different ways. The older galleries come from a time when colonialism was much less at the forefront of public consciousness, and they present the artefacts with little to no commentary on the colonial dynamic. Interestingly, some galleries, the wall text is also translated into languages likely to be spoken by people from the area where the artefacts are from, and I think this is a fairly old feature of the curation. So even if colonial aspects of the artefacts' provenance wasn't at the forefront, thought had been given to how the people who's heritage was on display might access the collection. The gallery of polynesian masks was clearly designed bespoke for the exhibit, in a time before wide-ranging post-colonial discourse, and they have gone the way of adding stickers asking pointed questions ("who is not in the frame here?" etc) which I find a fairly low-effort attempt to tick the decolonial box (although I'm sympathetic that it would be difficult to remodel this whole section). The section on South Tyrol asks interesting questions about the orientalisation of areas considered part of Europe. The most contemporary exhibits were then clearly designed with post-colonial ideas in mind. These were: an exhibit on salt mining in Chile (the salt industry made some Hamburg merchants very rich, see the Chilehaus), an exhibit on modern South Korea, and a temporary exhibit "Yurt! Jetzt!" on Central Asian nomadic people. The salt exhibit was particularly powerful, examining a colonial relationship of local importance in a way that felt really instructive. The sections on Korea and nomads had clearly been designed more collaboratively with members of those cultures, and felt more like cultural sharing than extraction. Naturally, these more modern exhibits were less rich in artefacts, since the usual means by which artefacts came to Hamburg in the past is no longer an acceptable way to build a collection.
Museum der Arbeit 19.01.25 Housed in an old factory in the former factory district of Barmbek, the Museum der Arbeit (Museum of Work) describes the history of work throughout industrialization. The museum has a dedicated section on the print industry, and various exhibits show how this work changed over time and how this affected workers. The general pattern that "a new technology threatened jobs, but workers protected themselves through unionization, although women were often sidelined" is a running theme to the point that it gets a bit repetitive (although it's obviosuly a key pattern to take away). Interestingly there is a part of the printworks that is in operation and you can even go on certain evenings to take classes and use the materials. For me this is a highlight of the curation, that they have taken the opportunity to bring the history alive with hands-on experiences while also keeping some heritage work alive. Other sections discuss various aspects of work more generally (e.g. uniforms, health and safety, post-war social democratic measures like compulsory insurance, etc.) and some sections focus on the rubber industry that the building was originally a factory for. The temporary exhibit was on the history of mail order, and was pretty fun. I didn't know how well-developed a mail-order system the DDR had!
Altonaer Museum 12.10.25 This museum charts the history of the district of Altona, which was originally a Danish borough before eventually becoming a part of Hamburg. Much of the museum looks at the fishing and seafaring which were Altona's main economy originally: from the early days of wodden shipbuilding through to the migrant workers from Spain and Turkey who came in the 20th centrry to work in the fish industry. It's interesting to imagine this borough which is now a part of Hamburg was once its own port in competition with the neighbouring port of Hamburg. If you like model ships, this is a good place for you, and there is also a collection of ships' figureheads and a section about historical shipbuilding. I unfortunately found the section about Altona through to the 19th century quite difficult to follow, as the texts here were exlusively in German and also the layout of that gallery did not feel very linear. On the top floor there is a "folk museum" section, with reconstructions of historical rural houses from 17th - 18th century northern Germany. This is interwoven with text and sound from the German novel Heimatmuseum about a curator of a folk museum who is driven to destroy the collection. There's also a section about animated films, although I wasn't sure if there was an Altona-specifc connection there. The museum is very kid-friendly, with multiple sections dedicated to children as well as clear thought about how to make the other sections child-accessible. There are regular children's book events hosted here too. In fact, I found the large numbers of boisterous kids coming out of one of those events quite a lot to contend with, so if you value a quieter museum experience it might be best to avoid those times.
Deutsches Hafenmuseum 19.10.25 The Deutsches Hafenmuseum (German Harbour Museum) is located south of the Elbe, near Hamburg's immense contemporary port operations. The museum is housed in a timber building which is a former warehouse. In the warehouse sections are various exhibits about the history of contemporary shipping. The period being charted starts back then things were arriving in barrels, bales and crates, and goes up to the present era of container shipping. This part has a nice homemade feel, and there are some little hands-on demos. In fact, lots of these exhibits seem touchable and it isn't toally clear what should or shouldn't be touched. A favourite artefact for me was a draftsman's board which would have been used in making blueprints for ships, where you could move the transparent rule around. The interior of the warehouse is just the start of the story, though: outside are various instalations, ranging from defunct cranes, to freight rolling stock, to some floating exhibits. These include a floating crane, where you can see how the operator would have lived on board (these are still in operation and it's amazing to me that they stay upright). The jewel in the crown is a ship called the Peking, however you have to book this in advance and I failed to notice this before visiting, so wasn't able to go on board. However, all was not lost! A little further up on another pontoon were some boats looked after by the Stiftung Hamburg Maritim (Hamburg Maritime Foundation). We were able to look around their steam ship Schaarhorn, which they have maintained in a seagoing (and steam-powered!) state. Seeing this volunteer-run piece of living history was really cool!
Zaubermuseum Bellachini 31.10.25 Another year, another "see for free". The Zaubermuseum (magic museum) was the first of Hamburg's many small-scale museums I have visited. It more or less consists of a single room. It has some antique magic kits on display as well as vintage posters. When I visited the exhibit was dedicated to a well-known 20th century German celebrity magician with the stage name Kalanag. I didn't get much from the exhibit, and was intrigued if there was even a Hamburg connection. On taking a look at his Wikipedia page, it seems he was an active participant in the Nazi regime, becoming involved in propaganda efforts and producing an anti-semitic film. It was disappointing that these complicated histories were not presented, although I have to be realistic about what can be expected from a museum which seems to be run by enthusiasts rather than trained curators. Also, I'm not sure if the museum us usually dedicated to Kalanag or if this is a temporary feature (pictures on the website have way less Kalanag stuff).
Museum für Kunst & Gewerbe 31.10.25 The Museum für Kunst & Gewerbe (Museum of Art & Industry) is located right across from the south entrance to Hamburg's main station. In this visit I just explored the gallery of Asian arts. The efforts to emphasise the interplay between form and function, and to draw out artefacts' origins in certain economic and historical moments (as well as to explore the processes, often colonial, which brought them to Hamburg) were really impressive. For example, the presentation of Japanese swords was able to effectively convey the importance of changing structures in production (increased specialisation and division of labour leading to the emergence of family workshops) in the development of distinctive styles of hilt. There were also admirable efforts to, where possible, allow us to touch examples of artefacts, helping to bring out the functional role of many of the pieces as things which were designed to be held and used. Prior to visiting, the marketing of this place hadn't appealed to me, but now that I've been to one of the galleries I am excited to return some time to see what else is in store.

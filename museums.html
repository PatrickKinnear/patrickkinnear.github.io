Museums in Hamburg

Kunsthalle 31.10.24 Visited on the ''see for free'' day of free entry so it was very busy, and made the mistake of trying to see everything. But the collection is impressive and laid out chronologically, with the main collection beginning with medieval Christian art and progressing through to the 20th century. I particularly liked the section on impressionism, where a former curator had brought leading impressionists to Hamburg to paint local scenes. Many of these were of shipping work around the Elbe, and I found the depictions of these quotidian work-life scenes in impressionist paintings really interesting. There was also some Munch which was another highlight for me. I was less enamoured with the more recent modern art in the annex. The headline piece, Caspar David Friedrich's ''Wanderer,'' was out on loan. For this, and to digest the collection in a more leisurely way, the collection bears returning to. Luckily there is a day of late- and free-opening once a month.
MARKK 31.10.24 The Museum am Rotherbaum, also called MARKK, is an anthropological museum. It is interesting not just for its collections, but also in a meta sense as reflecting current changes in curation as decolonial approaches become more important. What is interesting about the museum is that it has several galleries which were themselves curated in different historical moments, and thus frame the material in different ways. The older galleries come from a time when colonialism was much less at the forefront of public consciousness, and they present the artefacts with little to no commentary on the colonial dynamic. Interestingly, some galleries, the wall text is also translated into languages likely to be spoken by people from the area where the artefacts are from, and I think this is a fairly old feature of the curation. So even if colonial aspects of the artefacts' provenance wasn't at the forefront, thought had been given to how the people who's heritage was on display might access the collection. The gallery of polynesian masks was clearly designed bespoke for the exhibit, in a time before wide-ranging post-colonial discourse, and they have gone the way of adding stickers asking pointed questions ("who is not in the frame here?" etc) which I find a fairly low-effort attempt to tick the decolonial box (although I'm sympathetic that it would be difficult to remodel this whole section). The section on South Tyrol asks interesting questions about the orientalisation of areas considered part of Europe. The most contemporary exhibits were then clearly designed with post-colonial ideas in mind. These were: an exhibit on salt mining in Chile (the salt industry made some Hamburg merchants very rich, see the Chilehaus), an exhibit on modern South Korea, and a temporary exhibit "Yurt! Jetzt!" on Central Asian nomadic people. The salt exhibit was particularly powerful, examining a colonial relationship of local importance in a way that felt really instructive. The sections on Korea and nomads had clearly been designed more collaboratively with members of those cultures, and felt more like cultural sharing than extraction. Naturally, these more modern exhibits were less rich in artefacts, since the usual means by which artefacts came to Hamburg in the past is no longer an acceptable way to build a collection.
